Yes. This is very common in senior-level interviews, and the way you respond can matter almost as much as knowing the answer.
For your Senior .NET / Azure / Engineering Lead interviews, don't panic or try to bluff. The goal is to demonstrate honesty, confidence, problem-solving ability, and willingness to learn.
1. If you know it, but you can't remember at that moment
Don't say:
❌ "I don't know."
Instead:
"I'm familiar with this concept, but I can't recall the exact details at the moment. Let me explain what I remember about it..."
Then explain whatever you genuinely know.
Example
Interviewer:
"What is the difference between Cosmos DB consistency levels?"
You:
"I'm familiar with Cosmos DB consistency models, but I can't recall all the exact details at the moment. I do remember that they provide different trade-offs between consistency, latency, and availability. I would need to verify the exact behavior of each level before implementing it in production."
This is much better than guessing.
________________________________________
2. If you genuinely don't know
Be honest, but don't stop at "I don't know."
Use:
"I haven't worked directly with that technology/concept, so I don't want to give you an incorrect answer. However, based on my experience with similar technologies, my understanding would be..."
If you can connect it to something you know, do so.
Example
Interviewer:
"Have you worked with Kubernetes?"
You:
"I haven't worked directly with Kubernetes in a production environment, so I don't want to claim hands-on experience. My primary cloud experience has been with Azure services such as Cosmos DB, Azure Functions, Azure Storage and Azure DevOps. I understand the purpose of Kubernetes for container orchestration, but I would need some hands-on experience to discuss it in depth."
That's a strong professional answer.
________________________________________
3. If you understand the concept but don't remember the terminology
This happens frequently.
Say:
"I know the concept you're referring to, but I'm not recalling the exact terminology. Let me explain it from my understanding."
Then explain it.
This is especially useful for design patterns, Azure services, C# features, and architectural concepts.
________________________________________
4. If the question is unclear
Don't immediately answer.
Ask the interviewer to clarify.
"Could you please clarify the scenario you're referring to?"
or:
"Are you asking this from a .NET implementation perspective or from an architecture perspective?"
This is actually a senior-level behavior.
For example:
Interviewer:
"How would you handle caching?"
You can ask:
"Would you like me to answer that for a single application instance, or for a distributed application running across multiple instances?"
Now you have demonstrated architectural thinking.
________________________________________
5. If you need a moment to think
It's perfectly acceptable to pause.
Use:
"That's a good question. Let me think about the best way to approach it."
Take a few seconds.
Then structure your answer.
Don't fill the silence with:
"Actually... basically... you know... I think..."
A short pause makes you look thoughtful rather than nervous.
________________________________________
6. If you give an answer and then realize it may be wrong
Correct yourself.
"Let me correct that. I believe I misspoke in my previous answer. The correct approach is..."
This is much better than continuing with an incorrect answer.
Senior developers are expected to be able to recognize and correct mistakes.

Acknowledge → Relate → Explain → Learn

I've worked with similar concepts in cloud-based applications, so I believe I could learn Kafka quickly if the project requires it."
That's a much stronger response than:

Yes. What you're experiencing is very common during interviews, especially when you have studied many topics. The problem usually isn't that you've forgotten the knowledge permanently. It's that you can recognize the concept when reading it, but you can't retrieve it quickly when you're under pressure.
For example, you may have read dependency injection 10 times. When you see:
"What is Dependency Injection?"
you think:

1. Stop rereading; start active recall
This is the biggest change I recommend.
❌ Current approach
You read:
Dependency Injection → definition → benefits → example → types
You feel:
"Yes, I understand this."
Then you move to the next topic.
But during the interview, there is nothing in front of you to trigger the memory.
✅ New approach
Close your notes and ask yourself:
"What is Dependency Injection?"
Try to answer without looking.
Even if you remember only:
"DI... dependency... loose coupling..."
That's good.
Then open your notes and check what you missed.
This process is called active recall, and it's much more useful for interview preparation than repeatedly reading the same material.

2. Use the "5-point recall" method
For every interview concept, don't memorize a long answer.
Remember just 5 things:
1. What?
What is it?
2. Why?
Why do we use it?
3. How?
How does it work?
4. Example
Can I give a simple example?
5. When?
When would I use it?
For example:
Dependency Injection
What?
A technique for providing dependencies to a class instead of creating them inside the class.
Why?
Loose coupling and better testability.
How?
Constructor injection, primarily.
Example?
Inject IRepository into a service.
When?
When a class depends on another service/component.
That's enough to reconstruct a good interview answer.
You don't need to memorize a 2-minute paragraph.

7. Use the "3-level answer" technique
Don't force yourself to produce a perfect answer immediately.
Level 1 — 15 seconds
Give the definition.
"Dependency Injection is a technique where dependencies are provided to a class rather than created inside it."
Level 2 — 30 seconds
Add the purpose.
"It helps us achieve loose coupling and makes the code easier to test and maintain."
Level 3 — 60–90 seconds
Add an example.
"For example, in ASP.NET Core, we can register an interface and its implementation in the DI container and inject it into a service through the constructor..."
This is much easier for your brain than trying to remember a complete 90-second answer immediately.

10. What to do when your mind goes blank in the interview
This is very important.
Suppose the interviewer asks:
"What are the SOLID principles?"
And suddenly your mind is blank.
Don't panic.
Take a breath.
Say:
"Yes, I'm familiar with SOLID principles. Let me recall them one by one."
Then start with whatever comes to mind:
"S is Single Responsibility..."
Once you say the first one, your memory often starts retrieving the others.
If you remember only three:
"I can recall Single Responsibility, Open/Closed, and Dependency Inversion. I'm missing the exact order of the remaining principles at the moment."
That's much better than freezing.
